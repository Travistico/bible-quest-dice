# Bible Quest Dice — Design Document

**Version 0.1 — working draft**
*(Working title only. Avoid "Story Cubes" in any published name — see Notes on IP.)*

---

## 1. Core Concept

Nine picture dice are rolled into a shared pool. Players dwell on the roll, then take turns claiming **any number of relevant dice** and connecting them to a real story, passage, or lesson from the Bible — drawing out what it means.

The game generates **recall, interpretation, and application**. It never generates new scripture.

### The guiding distinction

| Register | What it is | In this game? |
|---|---|---|
| **Canon** | Fixed, closed, not to be added to | Never |
| **Exposition** | Drawing out meaning already in the text | Yes |
| **Illustration** | An invented scene teaching a fixed truth (a parable, a sermon anecdote) | Yes |
| **Imagination** | Invention in service of a teaching | Yes — see Story mode |
| **Application** | Bringing it to bear on your own life | Yes |

Anything a player invents is **scaffolding**, not scripture. It's disposable — the same way a sermon illustration, a Sunday-school object lesson, or a child's retelling at the flannelgraph is disposable. Nobody mistakes any of those for canon, and nobody will mistake a story built from dice either.

The safety of the game never depended on every turn carrying a moral — it rests on the imagery, the world, and the destination all being scriptural, plus the standing rule that play closes back in the text. Story mode is directed at a core teaching not to keep it safe, but because that's where its value is: a story built to carry forgiveness or humility teaches something, while a story built from nothing teaches nothing.

### On correspondences

The optional inner-meaning layer draws on Swedenborg's doctrine of correspondences, which assigns *stable* spiritual meanings to natural images. This is what prevents free-floating invention: a roll isn't a blank prompt, it's a small text in a fixed symbolic vocabulary.

**Important pairing:** love (good) and truth are not the same thing — they are two halves of a pair. Love/good corresponds to *warmth, fire, bread, sun*. Truth/faith corresponds to *light, water, wine, lamp*. Their joining is the "marriage of good and truth," and it becomes a scoring mechanic (see Modes: Marriage).

**Presentation note:** this is one interpretive tradition (New Church / Swedenborgian), not a universally held reading. The rulebook should present it as *a way of seeing*, not the settled meaning of scripture.

## 2. The Core Nine Dice (54 faces)

Each die covers a different domain, so almost every roll yields water **and** light **and** a creature **and** a place **and** a person — the spread that lets a scene cohere.

### Die 1 — Living Waters
`water/waves` · `wine` · `well` · `river` · `rain cloud` · `cup`

> *Truth in its forms.* Water = truth. Wine = truth from good. Well = its source. River = doctrine flowing. Cloud/rain = the Word in its outer sense. Cup = your own reception.

### Die 2 — Light & Fire
`sun` · `flame` · `oil lamp` · `star` · `rainbow` · `lightning`

> *Love and its wisdom.* Sun = the Lord / Divine love. Flame = love. Lamp and star = truth, faith. Rainbow = the state of the reborn (Noah). Lightning = Divine truth in power.

### Die 3 — Bread & Harvest
`loaves` · `wheat sheaf` · `grapes on the vine` · `olive branch` · `honeycomb` · `salt`

> *Good.* Bread = the good of love. Wheat = the church's harvest. Vine = the spiritual church. Olive = celestial good. Honey = the delight of good. Salt = the longing that binds truth to good.

### Die 4 — Flocks & Creatures
`lamb` · `dove` · `lion` · `serpent` · `fish` · `ox`

> *Affections.* Lamb = innocence. Dove = the holy, peace. Lion = truth in power. Serpent = the sensual (cunning, or the lowest natural). Fish = facts, natural truth. Ox = natural good.

### Die 5 — Places & States
`mountain` · `garden` · `wilderness` · `field` · `city gate` · `path`

> *States of the soul.* Mountain = love, nearness to the Lord. Garden = intelligence (Eden). Wilderness = temptation. Field = the church as to good. City gate = doctrine (New Jerusalem). Path = "the way," truth leading to good.

### Die 6 — Green & Growing
`tree` · `seed` · `fig` · `lily` · `palm branch` · `thorns`

> *Perception and its dangers.* Tree = the whole person, perception. Seed = truth sown. Fig = natural good. Lily = pure trust. Palm = victory, the good of truth. Thorns = the cares that choke.

### Die 7 — People & Callings
`shepherd` · `king` · `child` · `bride` · `servant` · `elder`

> *The human as symbol.* Shepherd = one who leads to good. King = Divine truth reigning. Child = innocence. Bride = the church joined to the Lord. Servant = truth in willing obedience. Elder = wisdom.

### Die 8 — Holy Things
`altar` · `tablets of the law` · `temple` · `incense` · `bread-and-cup` · `menorah`

> *Worship.* Altar = worship from good. Tablets = Divine truth, covenant. Temple = heaven and the Lord (and the reborn person). Incense = grateful worship rising. Bread-and-cup = good and truth together. Menorah = Divine truth giving light.

### Die 9 — Way & Passage
`door` · `key` · `boat` · `ladder` · `scales` · `yoke`

> *Entering and crossing over.* Door = the Lord as the way in ("I am the door"). Key = power to open heaven. Boat = what carries you through, the church that saves. Ladder = heaven joined to earth (Jacob). Scales = judgment and equity. Yoke = conjunction and service ("take my yoke upon you").

> **Changed from earlier draft:** `hand` moved off this die to the new Senses & Body die; `yoke` added in its place. This tightened Die 9 from a mixed bag into a single coherent theme.

## 3. Companion Dice

Optional components, each unlocking a deeper gear. None required for a first game.

### Die A — Vessels & Instruments *(objects)*
`sword` · `clay vessel` · `scroll` · `garment/robe` · `net` · `crown`

> Sword = Divine truth fighting falsity. Vessel = the receptacle (treasure in earthen vessels; the widow's oil jars; the jars at Cana). Scroll = the Word itself. Garment = truths clothing good (Joseph's coat, the prodigal's robe, the hem touched). Net = teaching that gathers (fishers of men, the dragnet). Crown = wisdom, victory, reward — and the crown of thorns.

### Die B — Senses & Body
`eye` · `ear` · `tongue` · `hand` · `foot` · `heart`

> Eye = the understanding ("the eye is the lamp of the body"; the man born blind). Ear = obedience, hearkening ("he who has ears"; Malchus; "speak, for your servant hears"). Tongue = confession, doctrine, speech (Pentecost; taming the tongue; the coal touching Isaiah's lips). Hand = power (the withered hand; laying on of hands; Moses' hands held up; the writing on the wall). Foot = the natural degree, walking in the way (washing the disciples' feet; "a lamp unto my feet"; shaking off the dust). Heart = the will, love ("create in me a clean heart"; heart of stone to heart of flesh).

**Why this die is strong:** it's the only one that points at *the player*. Every other die names something out in the world; these name the faculties doing the reading. Natural fit for Reflect mode.

### Die C — Mode Die (core)
`GLEAN` · `FOCUS` · `STORY LAW` · `REFLECT` · `QUESTION` · `RIDDLE`

Six faces, all settled. **Glean — the base mode — is face one.**

### Die D — Mode Die (deep)
`INNER MEANING` · `CHAIN` · `THREAD` · `SPAN` · `MARRIAGE` · `TWO-LENS`

### Die E — Word Die *(inner meaning)*
`LOVE` · `TRUTH` · `GOOD` · `FAITH` · `INNOCENCE` · `WISDOM`

Names an inner-meaning *target*; the image dice supply the natural correspondences that "spell" it. Roll TRUTH beside `water` and `lamp` → show how these embody truth, then find the passage (the woman at the well; the wise virgins).

> **Variant to test:** split into two word dice — one of *good* concepts, one of *truth* concepts — so a roll can produce both at once. That pairing **is** the marriage of good and truth, and could be the highest-value turn in the game.

---

### Die F — The Decalogue *(d10)*
`Alone` · `Idol` · `Name` · `Rest` · `Parents` · `Life` · `Marriage` · `Property` · `Witness` · `Desire`

Ten-sided, and **words rather than images** — deliberately. Nine of the ten obvious pictures are already taken by content dice (`sword`, `scales`, `tongue`, `eye`, `flame`), and unlike a content face this die must point at exactly one thing.

Words name the **subject matter**, not the vice — *Property*, not *Theft*; *Desire*, not *Covet*. Neutral nouns open a question instead of delivering a verdict.

**No numerals.** Jewish, Catholic and Protestant traditions number these differently; printing numbers silently picks a side. The ten words survive across all of them.

`Rest` also fills a real gap — there is no sabbath imagery anywhere in the core 54.

Drawn at the start of a **Story Law** turn, and optionally as a Reflect prompt.

## 3b. Reference Materials

**The deciding test for cards vs. booklet: do you *draw* it, or *look it up*?**

Anything drawn in play needs to be a card, because randomization is the point. Anything consulted needs to be a booklet, because you need to find one specific entry fast. That split resolves cleanly here.

### The Teaching Deck *(cards — drawn)*

**The second draw source for Story Law**, alongside the d10 — and an optional anchor for any mode. Where the d10 gives the fixed Ten, this deck gives the wider commands: *love one another, forgive as you are forgiven, do not be afraid, feed my sheep.* Roughly 24–40 cards.

**Each card carries:**
- The teaching — *Forgiveness*
- 3–4 verse references where scripture speaks to it
- One or two figures who embody it and one who fails at it — *Joseph and his brothers; the unforgiving servant*
- A discussion prompt

**Starter list:** Forgiveness · Humility · Pride · Betrayal · Mercy · Envy · Repentance · Obedience · Gratitude · Hospitality · Justice · Patience · Courage · Faithfulness · Generosity · Trust · Fear · Anger · Honesty · Perseverance · Contentment · Compassion · Wisdom · Sacrifice

**Variant worth testing:** draw the teaching *before* rolling, so players hunt the dice for imagery that serves it rather than the reverse. Harder, and probably more rewarding.

**Expansion hook:** a Lent deck, an Advent deck, a Beatitudes deck, a Fruits of the Spirit deck. Same component, different cards.

### The Symbol Reference *(booklet — consulted)*

One entry per face — 54 for the core nine, plus companion dice. Cards would be the wrong format: nobody wants to shuffle through 54 loose cards hunting for "lamp."

**Entry format** — verses first, correspondence second, and a question last:

> **LAMP** *(Die 2 — Light & Fire)*
> **Where it appears:** Psalm 119:105 · Matthew 5:15 · Matthew 6:22 · Matthew 25:1–13 · Revelation 21:23
> **May suggest:** truth, faith, the Word lighting the way, watchfulness
> **Inner meaning:** truth from good — faith enlightened by love
> **Ask:** what fuels it? whose lamp went out, and why?

> **WATER** *(Die 1 — Living Waters)*
> **Where it appears:** Genesis 1:2 · Genesis 7 · Exodus 14 · Exodus 17:6 · John 4 · John 7:38 · Revelation 22:1
> **May suggest:** truth, cleansing, chaos, deliverance, thirst
> **Inner meaning:** truth
> **Ask:** is this water that drowns, or water that saves?

**Three rules for writing the booklet:**

1. **Verses before interpretation.** Players should reach scripture before they reach anyone's reading of it.
2. **"May suggest," never "means."** A definition re-pins the image and undoes the decision to keep names off the dice. Plural, open phrasing preserves the multivalence the whole game runs on.
3. **End every entry with a question, not an answer.** Keeps the booklet a door rather than a verdict.

**Risk to watch:** a reference book can quietly become an authority — "let me check what it says" replacing "what do you see?" Mitigate with the three rules above, an explicit note that the lists are not exhaustive, and blank space for the group to add their own verses and readings over time. A well-worn booklet full of a group's own marginalia is the ideal outcome.

**In the app:** tap a die, see its entry. This also gives you free instrumentation — logging which symbols get looked up most tells you exactly which icons are unclear and need redrawing before physical production.

## 4. Component Visual Design

### Two visual languages

The game uses **two deliberately different visual vocabularies**, because the dice do two different jobs:

| | Content dice | Mode & word dice |
|---|---|---|
| **Job** | Carry meaning to be interpreted | Give an instruction |
| **Needs** | Multivalence — one image, many readings | Exactly one meaning, read instantly |
| **Language** | Pictorial, representational | Abstract, geometric |
| **Words on face?** | Never | Possibly (see below) |

The reasoning that keeps words *off* the content dice is the same reasoning that argues for a different treatment *on* the mode die. A label pins an image to one meaning — fatal for `dove`, ideal for `focus`.

### Why abstract marks for modes

Pictorial mode symbols would **collide with content faces**:

- a gleaning sheaf ↔ `wheat sheaf` (Die 3)
- an eye for Focus ↔ `eye` (Senses die)
- a scroll for Story ↔ `scroll` (Objects die)
- a mirror for Reflect ↔ a plausible Objects-die face

Geometric marks can't collide, because nothing else in the game is geometric.

**Draft marks (core mode die):** Glean = dots gathering into an arc · Focus = concentric rings · Story Law = a winding line meeting a horizontal rule · Reflect = a form mirrored across a line · Question = `?` · Riddle = a mark partly concealed

**Learnability:** six symbols, read once per round. Most tables will have them by the end of session one. Low risk — very different from asking players to learn 54 content faces.

### Differentiating the dice

**Do not rely on color alone.** Roughly 1 in 12 men has red-green color deficiency; if hue is the only thing separating core-mode from deep-mode from word dice, those players lose the distinction. Pair color with a second cue:

- **Size** — mode dice slightly larger
- **Finish** — matte vs. gloss, engraved vs. printed
- **Corner radius** — sharper or rounder
- **Visual language** — pictorial vs. geometric (already a non-color cue, and free)

**Proposed color families**

| Component | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Core nine | One shared color (bone/ivory) — reinforces that they're a single pool |
| Objects & Senses | A related second shade — identifiable when added, still reads as content |
| Mode dice (core / deep) | Strongly distinct deep ink, geometric marks, size difference |
| Word die | Third treatment — the only die carrying actual words |

### Words on the mode die?

**Recommendation for v1:** symbol **plus a small word** beneath. A mode face is read once per round by whoever is running it — ambiguity there costs the whole round, not just one turn. The cost is minor aesthetically; the failure mode it removes is not.

**Counterweight:** wordless dice are language-independent. A symbol-only mode die ships to every market unchanged; a worded one needs a print run per language. This matters more at scale than at prototype. A wordless "deluxe" edition is a natural later release once the symbol vocabulary is established.

**App advantage:** symbol sets are free to test digitally. Ship a words-on/words-off toggle and instrument it — real data on whether the marks are learnable, before committing to a physical print run.

## 5. Universal Table Rules

These apply in **every** mode.

1. **Any amount of dice.** Claim one, or claim six. This is what makes the game generous: a newcomer and a lifelong reader can play the same roll at the same table without anyone feeling behind.
2. **Never force a die.** If it doesn't fit, leave it standing.
3. **Naming a set-aside counts.** Saying *why* a die doesn't belong to your story is a legitimate, scoring contribution. This keeps honest restraint as valuable as clever fitting.
4. **Close in the text.** Every turn ends by pointing at an actual story, passage, or teaching — book and chapter where possible.
5. **The table may override the rolled mode** by agreement. Groups do this instinctively; the rules may as well bless it.

## 6. Modes

### Base mode

**GLEAN** — *Gather what's usable; leave the rest standing.*
The heart of the game. Four beats:

1. **Claim** — take any number of dice that genuinely relate.
2. **Connect** — name the passage, story, or teaching.
3. **Draw out** — say what the lesson or meaning is. *(This beat is what makes it more than trivia.)*
4. **Set aside** *(optional)* — name a die you deliberately left, and why.

### Core modes

| Mode | Goal | How it plays |
|---|---|---|
| **Focus** | Depth over breadth, and an opening to learn | One die is chosen and stays on the table. The player who claims it says what it brings to mind — a story, a meaning, why it stood out — and then **the floor opens**. Anyone may add what they know, ask what they don't, or offer a passage the first speaker didn't reach. The single image is a well the whole table draws from. Best opening mode: anyone can say something about a lamp, and nobody has to say everything. |
| **Story Law** | Build a story that fulfils a command of God | Draw the command **first**, then roll — the constraint bites before you see your images. Invent a character and a scene in which that command is fulfilled or given up. Drawn from either the **d10** (the fixed Ten) or the **Teaching Deck** (the wider commands — forgiveness, love one another, mercy). Runs **solo** (one teller takes the whole roll) or **relay** (passed around, each player adding a die). You are telling a story about someone, not examining yourself, which keeps the mode out of finger-pointing. The register of the parable. See full write-up below. |
| **Reflect** | What scripture says we should be working on — and optionally, where you are in it | Claim a face and speak to it in three beats: **what** is being worked on *biblically*, **why** it matters *biblically*, **how** scripture says to go about it. The Bible is the subject; the player is not. A fourth beat — *where I am in this* — is always available and never required. |
| **Question** | Open discussion without needing the answer | Claim dice and *pose a question* the roll raises rather than answering one. The asker may not answer first. Anyone can play; the table does the work. See full write-up below. |
| **Riddle** | One clue, one guess, everyone at once | The clue-giver picks a story, lays out any number of dice, and gives **one word**. Everyone writes a single guess; all reveal together. The clue-giver scores best when *some but not all* get it. The only simultaneous face on the die. See full write-up below. |

### Other mode candidates *(not on the core die — held for expansions and variants)*

| Candidate | Goal | How it plays |
|---|---|---|
| **Witness** | Enter the story from inside | Claim a die as your vantage point and tell the story from that perspective — the dove in the ark, the ox in the stable, the jar at Cana. Turns recall into something felt. |
| **Before & After** | Test context, not just identification | Claim dice, name the moment — then say what happened just before or just after. Knowing where a scene sits in the arc is deeper than naming it. |
| **Odd One Out** | Reward restraint | Find the die that *doesn't* belong with the others and defend why. Inverts the game and reinforces the set-aside rule that's already its spine. |
| **Relay** | Cooperative recall | One player begins a story, then passes; the next continues, adding a die. Team-building by construction — nobody has to carry the whole passage alone. |
| **Verse Hunt** | Get the actual book open | Race or collaborate to find a real verse containing your image. The only mode that puts scripture physically in players' hands. |
| **Teach It** | Simplicity as mastery | Explain your dice's story as if to a child who's never heard it. Hard in the best way, and immediately useful for anyone who actually teaches. |
| **Cast** | See the roll as a scene | Assign dice to roles — who here is the lamp, who is the thorns, who is the shepherd? Bridges naturally into the correspondence layer. |
| **Psalm** | Reach the poetic books | Connect dice to a psalm, proverb, or song rather than a narrative. Corrects the game's natural pull toward story-only recall. |
| **Blessing** | Turn the roll outward | Use your dice to speak a scriptural blessing or encouragement over another player. Unscored, and possibly the warmest thing the game can do. |

**Strongest of these for a second look:** *Odd One Out* (most distinct from anything on the core die, and it reinforces the set-aside rule that's already the game's spine) and *Relay* (best fit for the team-building angle). *Blessing* may work better as a closing ritual for a session than as a rolled face.

### Question mode — full write-up

**The core move:** every other mode rewards what you know. Question mode rewards what you *noticed and can't resolve*. A newcomer who rolls `serpent` and asks "why was it in the garden at all — who let it in?" has contributed more than someone who correctly names Genesis 3. This is the only mode where not knowing is an asset.

**Why it belongs in a Bible game specifically.** Question-asking is native to the text, not imported pedagogy. God's first words after the fall are a question — *"Where are you?"* Jesus taught by question constantly: *who do you say that I am*, *which of these was a neighbour*, *why do you call me good* — and often answered a question with one. Job ends with God asking dozens in a row and resolving almost none. The game isn't inventing a technique here; it's using one the text models throughout.

**Turn structure**
1. **Claim** any dice that prompted the question.
2. **Ask** — one question, arising from those dice.
3. **Open the floor.** The asker may not answer first.
4. *(Optional)* **Ground it** — someone finds where scripture speaks to it, if it does.
5. **Let it stand if unresolved.** Not every question gets closed.

**Kinds of questions it produces** — worth listing in the rulebook so players see the range:

- *Text* — what actually happens here? (`ladder` → why a ladder, and why did Jacob see it at that particular moment?)
- *Character* — why did she do that? what did he think was going to happen?
- *Meaning* — why this image, and why does it keep recurring? (bridges directly into Thread and Inner Meaning)
- *Connection* — why does the same picture show up in two places this far apart?
- *Honest / hard* — why did God ask Abraham for Isaac? Doubt is permitted here, and permitting it is the point.
- *Wonder* — no answer expected. The natural register for children.

**Where it's useful**

- **Small groups.** Solves the leader's perennial problem of finding a discussion starter. The roll plus the questions reveal what the group actually wants to talk about, rather than the leader guessing.
- **Mixed knowledge.** Removes the quiet test-anxiety of recall modes. A question can't be wrong, which changes the temperature of the whole table.
- **Teaching prep.** A teacher rolls against a passage they're preparing and collects the questions it raises — a fast way to anticipate what a class will actually ask.
- **Seekers and mixed-faith tables.** The least presumptuous mode in the game. It requires neither belief nor biblical literacy to participate fully.
- **Solo.** Write the question in the journal, then go hunting. A whole devotional practice on its own.

**The standing questions list.** Keep a running page of questions the table couldn't resolve, carried between sessions. It gives continuity across meetings, it models intellectual honesty, and returning to an old question months later — sometimes answerable now — is genuinely rewarding. This may be the single best artifact the game produces.

**Risks to watch in playtesting**
- A question can be a Trojan horse for a hobby-horse or a grievance. Mitigate: the question must arise from the claimed dice, and one question per turn.
- Discussion can eat the whole session. Mitigate: time-box, or cap it at two or three Question turns per evening.
- In some groups it may drift toward debate. Mitigate: the standing questions list gives unresolved things a place to *go*, rather than needing to be won.

**Scoring:** probably unscored. If a group wants points, score the question that generated the most discussion (table vote) rather than the one best answered.

**Overlap note:** now that Focus includes open discussion, Focus and Question sit close together. The clean distinction: **Focus starts from what someone knows or notices; Question starts from what someone doesn't.** Focus opens a well and everyone draws from it; Question opens a gap and everyone reaches into it. Worth confirming in playtest that the two feel different at the table — if they don't, merge them and free a die face.

### Reflect mode — full write-up

**The shape.** The anchor is scripture, not the player. Three beats, all answered from the Bible — with a fourth that belongs to the person and is always optional.

1. **What** — what is this image about, biblically? What does it name that a person has to contend with or grow in?
2. **Why** — why does scripture treat it as mattering? What's at stake in the text?
3. **How** — what does the Bible actually counsel? Which practices, which examples, which passages?
4. **Where I am in it** *(optional, volunteered, never asked for)* — the player steps into the light alongside the lesson if they want to.

**Worked examples**

> `thorns` — **What:** the cares of the world, the deceitfulness of riches, desire for other things, choking the word (Mark 4:18–19). **Why:** the seed wasn't bad and the soil wasn't barren — this is growth prevented, fruitlessness after real hearing. **How:** *do not be anxious… seek first the kingdom* (Matt 6); *lay aside every weight* (Heb 12:1).

> `yoke` — **What:** what you're bound to and pulling with (Matt 11:29–30; 2 Cor 6:14). **Why:** a yoke sets your direction and your pace — you cannot go where your yoke-partner isn't going. **How:** the counsel isn't to throw the yoke off but to *exchange* it. His is easy, the burden light, and the instruction is to learn from him.

> `wilderness` — **What:** the dry stretch — Israel's forty years, Christ's forty days. **Why:** scripture treats wilderness as formative rather than punitive: to humble, to test, to reveal what is in the heart (Deut 8:2). **How:** fed daily and only daily; the Word above bread; endurance rather than escape.

**Why anchoring it this way is better.** Three reasons. It keeps the mode inside the game's own rule that every turn closes in the text — the old version was the one face that could drift entirely into self-talk. It makes the mode *teach*: you leave knowing what scripture says about anxiety or bondage or dryness, not only how you feel about it. And paradoxically it produces **more** honest disclosure, not less: when the spotlight is optional and secondary, people volunteer for it. When it's the point, they perform or withdraw.

**Rules — these govern the optional fourth beat**

1. **Nobody is ever asked.** The fourth beat is volunteered or it doesn't happen. No going around the circle, no "and what about you?"
2. **No fixing.** If someone does step into the light, the table listens. No advice, no solutions, no scripture aimed *at* them unless they ask. This is the rule groups break most, and breaking it is what teaches people never to volunteer again.
3. **First person only.** You speak about your own life, never another player's.
4. **What's said here stays here.** Said aloud at the start of a session, not just printed in the rulebook.
5. **End in grace.** The turn closes on what scripture answers with, not on the problem.

**Group size.** Because the default turn is about the text rather than the player, this now works at twelve as well as at four — the mode teaches at any size, and only the optional fourth beat is small-group in character. Large groups will simply see it volunteered less often, which is fine.

**Relation to the silent examination practice.** These are opposites and both should exist: the examination practice is solo and unspoken, Reflect is deliberately shared. Don't merge them.

**Unscored.** Obviously.

### Story Law mode — full write-up

**Why it earns a core slot.** Every other mode works in the narrative or interpretive register. Nothing else engages the **imperative** register — and picture dice pull hard toward story and image, which means law, precept, and instruction get skipped by the entire system as designed. Story Law is the only face that reaches that material.

**Two draw sources** — these set the tone and the difficulty

- **The d10 (Decalogue).** Ten fixed targets, sharp and structural. Best for study groups and for teaching the Ten.
- **The Teaching Deck.** The wider commands — *love one another, forgive as you are forgiven, be merciful, welcome the stranger, do not be afraid.* Warmer, many more targets, far less legal in feel. These are commandments in the text's own terms: *a new commandment I give you.* Best for mixed groups, families, and anyone for whom the Decalogue framing lands cold.

Either deck can be drawn; a table may also shuffle both and take what comes.

**Two ways to tell it**

- **Solo.** One teller takes the whole roll and lands the command themselves. Tight and quick, good for small groups.
- **Relay.** The first player opens the story with one die and passes. Each player adds a die and continues, building on what came before — a player may reinterpret what's been said, never overwrite it. The story ends when the command has been fulfilled or decisively broken, or when the remaining dice genuinely won't serve it. The table builds one story together and must *steer* toward the command collectively.

Relay is the party-scaled version: in a group of ten, solo telling leaves nine people waiting, while relay gives everyone a turn inside a single story.

**Relay risks and fixes**

- *Incoherence as it passes.* Each addition must connect to what came before. Reinterpretation yes, contradiction no.
- *The last player gets stuck.* The universal set-aside rule applies — leftover dice can be left standing. Nobody is forced to use a die.
- *Nobody closes it.* Either the last player must land it, or any player may call the story closed once the command is fulfilled and the table agrees.

**Party variant:** draw the command *secretly* to the player who opens. Everyone else contributes blind, then guesses which command was being served. Turns the mode into a guessing game without spending a separate face on one.

**Two directions of play**

- **Command-first (anchored).** Draw the command, then roll and read the picture dice through it. The constraint is the point: you must find how *these* images speak to *that* law. Harder, and the version that best matches "limitation drives creativity."
- **Dice-first (open).** Roll the picture dice, then ask which commandment they raise. Easier, more discovery-flavoured, better for a first session.

**Two senses** *(natural and spiritual)*

| Roll | Natural | Spiritual |
|---|---|---|
| `hand` + `sword` | Do not murder | Hatred is murder (1 John 3:15); anger (Matt 5:21–22) |
| `bride` + `heart` | Do not commit adultery | Lust in the heart (Matt 5:28); idolatry as spiritual adultery |
| `tongue` + `scales` | Do not bear false witness | Distorting truth itself; false doctrine |
| `eye` + `field` + `ox` | Do not covet | Wanting another's state, not just their goods |

The Senses & Body die is the **best companion** for this mode — the law asks what you did with your body, and the spiritual sense asks what you did with the faculty behind it.

**Two ways to tell it** *(table picks, or the teller does)*

- **Solo telling.** One player takes the whole roll and tells the story start to finish. Tighter, more controlled, better for a strong storyteller or a small group.
- **Passed telling (relay).** The story moves around the table. Each player adds **one die** and one beat, building on what came before. Everyone is holding the same story, which makes it the most participatory thing on the core die.

**Rules for passed telling**

1. **One law for the whole chain.** Drawn at the start, shared by everyone. Each player's beat must move the story toward that law being kept or broken.
2. **The last player lands it.** Whoever adds the final die must resolve it — kept, or given up. That gives the chain a climax and a natural stopping rule.
3. **Kept or broken stays undecided** until the end. The chain discovers it. Deciding up front flattens the drama; letting it emerge means each player can push the character either way.
4. **A player may pass without adding.** The standing "never force a die" rule applies here too — if nothing in front of you fits, say why and pass. Naming the misfit still counts as a contribution.
5. **One lap, or until the dice run out.** Whichever comes first.

**Why this matters for group size.** Story Cubes scales to twelve players; so should this. Passed telling is the answer to that — in a large group, solo telling leaves eleven people waiting, while relay has everyone tracking the story and planning their beat. Consider making passed telling the **default above about six players**.

**Overlap note:** passed telling resembles **Chain** on the deep die, but they're doing opposite things. Chain sequences a *real* scriptural arc from memory; passed Story Law builds an *invented* one toward a law. Recall versus construction. Worth confirming they feel different in playtest.

**Ways to run a turn**

1. **Attach** — claim dice, name the law they touch.
2. **Which law is at stake?** — recall a story from the roll, then ask which commandment it turns on. Richest version. Naboth's vineyard breaks coveting, false witness, murder, and theft in one episode; David and Bathsheba nearly as many.
3. **Kept, not broken** — who in scripture *kept* this law? Joseph fleeing Potiphar's wife. Daniel refusing the king's gods. Naboth refusing to sell his inheritance. Counteracts the mode's pull toward accusation.
4. **The intensification** — take the law to the heart level as Jesus does in Matthew 5: *you have heard it said… but I say.*
5. **Two tables** — sort claimed dice into love-of-God (laws 1–4) and love-of-neighbour (5–10). The Decalogue is already built this way, and Jesus' summary maps straight onto the split.
6. **Hardest law** — which of the ten does this roll say *least* about? Inverts the mode; pairs with the set-aside rule.

**Three standing rules**

1. **First person only.** You name where a law touches *your* life. Never another player's. Without this the mode becomes finger-pointing within one session.
2. **Always end in grace.** After naming where a law is broken, name where scripture answers it with mercy. David breaks most of the Decalogue and Psalm 51 is the reply. Without this beat the mode ends on guilt every single time.
3. **No scoring.** Points would turn conviction into a contest.

**Risks**
- Most likely mode to turn moralistic — mitigated by rules 1 and 2.
- Ten targets against 54 faces gets repetitive faster than expected. Release valve: a **wider imperatives** variant (any command in scripture — *go, follow me, do not be afraid, feed my sheep, consider the lilies*), which trades tidy structure for a much larger target set.
- **Tonal balance:** with Wild gone, Story now carries all the levity on the core die. Watch in playtest whether the core six feels too weighty.

### Riddle mode — full write-up

**The problem it had to solve.** The multivalence that makes Glean generous makes encoding nearly impossible. `lamb` + `shepherd` + `hills` is David, or the lost sheep, or the nativity, or Psalm 23. Pure dice-laying is unguessable.

**The fix is the clue.** One word, alongside the dice. Add *lost* to those three faces and four candidates collapse to one. The dice carry the imagery; the clue does the disambiguating the dice can't.

**Turn structure**

1. **Choose** — the clue-giver picks a story the roll suggests.
2. **Lay out** — any number of dice.
3. **Clue** — exactly one word. No proper nouns (they trivialize it).
4. **Guess** — everyone writes a single guess, privately.
5. **Reveal** — all at once.

**The scoring rule that makes it work.** The clue-giver scores best when **some but not all** guess right. Everybody gets it → too easy, no points. Nobody gets it → too obscure, no points. This is Dixit's mechanism, and it removes any incentive to stump the table. Generosity becomes self-enforcing, and calibration becomes the actual game.

**Sample clues, weakest to strongest**

| Dice | Clue | Story | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| `boat` · `dove` · `rainbow` | *promise* | Noah | Too easy — everyone gets it, no score |
| `honeycomb` · `lion` | *sweet* | Samson | Good — rewards knowing an odd corner |
| `well` · `cup` · `water` | *stranger* | The woman at the well | Ideal — gettable if you know it |
| `bread` · `wilderness` · `serpent` | *forty* | The temptation | Clue carries most of the weight; fine |

**Why it belongs in this game specifically.** Riddling is biblically native. Samson poses the archetype at his wedding feast — *out of the strong came something sweet* — and Proverbs names understanding a proverb and a riddle among the book's stated purposes. The mode isn't importing a party mechanic; it's using one the text already contains.

**Why it earns the sixth face.** Every other core mode is sequential — one person speaks, everyone waits. At twelve players that's dead air for eleven. Riddle is the **only simultaneous face**: everyone thinks and guesses at once. It also has the lowest entry cost in the game, since guessing is far easier than recalling — a player who knows almost nothing can throw out "Jonah?" and be right.

**The one real cost:** the clue-giver needs to know a story well. It's the only seat requiring expertise. Mitigations: let the role go to whoever wants it rather than rotating strictly, and allow a **two-word clue** as an explicit easy mode.

### Deep modes

| Mode | Goal | How it plays |
|---|---|---|
| **Inner Meaning** | Read the spiritual sense | Don't tell a story — interpret the roll through the correspondence key. |
| **Chain** | Sequence scripture, not isolated bits | Players take turns adding **one** die to a thread that must stay within one story or arc. `wilderness` → `bread` (manna) → `water` (the rock) → `tablets` (Sinai). Cooperative. |
| **Thread** | Trace one symbol across the canon | Follow a single image from Genesis to Revelation — water: the flood → the Red Sea → the well → baptism → the river of life. |
| **Span** | See type and fulfillment | Score only by connecting an Old Testament story to a New Testament one through the same dice — the bronze serpent and the cross; Jonah and the resurrection; the Passover lamb and the Lamb of God. |
| **Marriage** | Unite good and truth | Pair a love/good image (`fire`, `sun`, `bread`, `oil`) with a truth image (`water`, `lamp`, `wine`) and show a passage joining them. The prize turn. |
| **Two-Lens** | See letter and spirit together | Read the same roll twice — once as recall, once as inner meaning — and the table judges whether the two illuminate each other. |

## 7. Scoring & Session Objectives

Scoring is **optional**. Reflect is best unscored — points would turn the optional fourth beat into a contest over who is most exposed. Riddle is the one mode whose scoring is **load-bearing** (see its write-up): the some-but-not-all rule is what makes clues generous.

### Alignment scoring (team-building)
Points for each die successfully aligned to one real story. Tables pool knowledge to claim the most dice for a single passage.

> ⚠️ **Tuning risk:** pure "most dice wins" quietly rewards stretching a die to fit and punishes the humbler reading. Counter-weight it: award points for a correctly named **set-aside** too, so restraint scores.

### Session objectives (cooperative)
- **Canon sweep** — as a table, touch as many books as you can in one session.
- **Seasonal** — during Lent, only wilderness-and-passion stories score.
- **Span bonus** — extra points for any turn linking Old and New Testament. Quietly teaches typology.
- **Rediscovery bonus** — if a player invents a scene in Story Law that turns out to already *be* a parable, award a bonus. It rewards exactly the instinct worth cultivating.

## 8. What Playtesting Has Shown So Far

From simulated rolls across the core nine:

- **The dice are story-dense.** Five random faces routinely land a specific, nameable episode. Nine faces reliably yield two or three full stories from one roll — exactly the "dwell, then everyone shares" experience.
- **Competition between stories is a feature.** `fire` + `altar` + `ox` claims Elijah at Carmel *and* Gideon. The "wait — this could also be…" moment is what drives good table discussion.
- **Not every die joins every roll, and that's healthy.** Leaving a die visibly unused models good interpretation: not everything has to fit.
- **Some faces dominate.** `lamb`, `altar`, `mountain`, `water`, `king` anchor a disproportionate share of stories. Watch for repetitiveness.

## 9. Open Questions

- [ ] **Should Objects and Senses be core rather than companion?** Currently the core is nine and these are add-ons. Making the game a core-11 would break the pocket-size format but both dice are strong.
- [ ] **Face balance.** Tally which faces get claimed and which sit dead across a real playtest run. Consider splitting the dominant symbols.
- [ ] **One word die or two?** (see Die E variant)
- [ ] **Two-Lens on the deep die** is the weakest face in the system — procedural where every other deep face has content. Candidate replacement: **Firstfruits** (name where you've received what an image stands for, then find where scripture speaks of God giving it).
- [ ] **Does Reflect need printed prompt cards**, or are the three beats enough?
- [ ] **Riddle clue length** — is one word right, or is two the real default?
- [ ] **Icon legibility.** Some correspondence icons are obscure (menorah, incense, ephod). Names go in the **reference booklet, not on the die faces** — a label pins the meaning, and multivalence is the engine of the whole game.
- [ ] **Kids' edition?** Glean / Focus / Riddle only; skip the correspondence layer and the d10 entirely.
- [ ] **Scoring calibration** for Span and Marriage — likely the rarest and highest-value turns.

**Phase 1 — Paper prototype.** Print the 54 faces on cards or write them on blank dice. Test Glean, Focus, and Question first; they need no correspondence knowledge and no artwork skill. Tally face usage.

**Phase 2 — Online playable build.** See section 11.

## 11. Online Build Spec *(for the test version)*

The goal of the online build is **not** to be the finished product. It is to answer questions the paper prototype can't answer at volume: which faces are dead, which modes get rerolled, whether the symbols are learnable.

### Minimum viable build

**Roll screen**
- Roll 9 core dice (toggle: 5 / 7 / 9).
- Roll the mode die alongside. Show the rolled mode's name, goal, and turn structure inline — don't make players remember six procedures.
- Toggle companion dice on/off: Objects, Senses, Word die, d10.
- Reroll mode die — this is the table-override rule, and **every use should be logged**.

**Per-face interaction**
- Tap a die → its Symbol Reference entry: verses first, "may suggest" second, inner meaning third, question last.
- Tap-to-claim: claimed dice visibly separate from the pool. **Set aside** is a distinct action from simply leaving a die unclaimed.

**Mode support, in build priority order**
1. **Glean** — claim, then free-text the passage and the lesson. Optional set-aside with reason.
2. **Riddle** — needs the most machinery and is the most testable: clue-giver lays dice and enters one word; others enter one guess; simultaneous reveal; auto-score on the some-but-not-all rule.
3. **Focus / Question** — single-die selection plus a text field. Question needs a **standing questions list** that persists between sessions.
4. **Story Law** — draw from the d10 or the Teaching Deck, then roll. Support both solo and passed telling (turn order, one die per beat, last player lands it).
5. **Reflect** — the three biblical beats as labelled fields, with the fourth beat visually optional and obviously skippable.

### Instrumentation — the actual point of building this

Log, per session:

- **Face claim frequency** — the single most valuable number. Confirms or kills the suspicion that `lamb`, `altar`, `mountain`, `water`, and `king` dominate.
- **Faces never claimed** — candidates for replacement before print.
- **Symbol lookups per face** — high lookups mean an unclear icon that needs redrawing.
- **Mode rerolls per face** — which modes tables actively avoid.
- **Riddle outcomes** — distribution of correct guesses per clue. This is what tells you whether one word is the right clue length.
- **Set-aside usage** — is the restraint rule actually used, or decorative?
- **Turn length by mode** — which modes eat the session.

### Deliberately out of scope for v1

Accounts, multiplayer sync, the correspondence deep modes (Chain, Thread, Span, Marriage), scoring beyond Riddle, and final art. Use placeholder icons with a **words-on / words-off toggle** — that toggle is itself an experiment, and it tells you whether the symbol set is learnable before you commit to a print run.

## 12. Notes on IP

*General information, not legal advice.*

- **Mechanics aren't copyrightable.** "Roll picture dice and tell a story" is free to use; picture storytelling dice predate Rory's Story Cubes anyway.
- **Avoid the name.** "Rory's Story Cubes" and likely "Story Cubes" function as brand names. Pick something distinctive.
- **Original art only.** Existing dice icons are copyrighted. Draw or commission every face.
- **Bible content is public domain** — the stories, characters, and imagery are ancient. But *modern translations* (NIV, ESV, NLT) are copyrighted. If quoting scripture on packaging or in the booklet, use the KJV or another public-domain translation, or check that translation's permissions.
- Before manufacturing at scale, a short consult with an IP attorney is a reasonable investment.
